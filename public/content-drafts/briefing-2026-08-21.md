## One Insight

The pediatric dentistry research surfaced a revenue pattern worth naming for Beverly's pitch toolkit: **the multi-appointment family effect**.

A pediatric dental practice that serves a family with three kids doesn't have one patient. It has three patients who share a decision-maker — the parent. When that parent thinks "dentist," all three kids' appointments are available in the same moment of attention. The problem is that practices almost never treat them as one unit. Emma gets a recall reminder. Marcus and Lily don't, because they weren't flagged in the same recall cycle.

The math compounds: a practice with 147 multi-child families, doing 1.4 additional appointments per family when they actively offer synchronized scheduling, generates nearly $10,000 in additional annual revenue from families already in their system. That's not a marketing campaign. That's pattern-matching on data they already have.

Beverly can use this framing in almost any service business: "How many of your customers have multiple people in your system? Because if you're reaching one and not the others, you're leaving a full appointment on the table every time you contact that family."

The HVAC version: "You serviced this customer's upstairs unit last spring. Did you follow up about the downstairs unit? They probably have one." The auto repair version: "This customer has two cars in the household. You have one on file. Does the second car know you exist?"

The principle is the same at every business Beverly walks into: the customer already trusts you. The question is whether you're collecting the full value of that trust, or only the piece they thought to ask for.
